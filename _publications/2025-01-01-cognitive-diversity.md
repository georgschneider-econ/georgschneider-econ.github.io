Many important decisions are made jointly by groups. While groups can draw on and discuss diverse perspectives, they are also susceptible to collective failures, such as herding. We investigate this tension by studying three features that distinguish joint from individual decision-making: composition, communication, and collaborative aggregation. Direct comparisons between joint and individual decisions cannot isolate these features. We separate them in an interactive online experiment in which retail investors construct portfolios to maximize returns and limit volatility in a randomly assigned decision mode. Adjacent modes differ from one another by a single feature. We find that the benefits of joint decision-making can be explained by composition alone, because pooling independent submissions reduces portfolio concentration and volatility. Communication undermines these benefits by reducing the diversity of members' inputs and encouraging riskier strategies that fail to increase returns on average. Requiring members to agree on one portfolio (collaborative aggregation) has little overall effect relative to pooling, but improves diversification when their beliefs differ. This decomposition reveals a cost of communication that prevents groups from making full use of diverse inputs.
